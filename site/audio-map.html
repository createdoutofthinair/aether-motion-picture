Map the song

CUE STUDY · ILLUSTRATIVE / 24 FPS

The eighteen-frame pause

The subject walks. Track alongside them.

Use the actual song file as the clock. Record its version, measured runtime, sample rate, timeline frame rate and start convention. Keep the original audio and place it once on the edit timeline. If the audio master is unavailable, develop an untimed treatment and flag timing as provisional. If only a lyric sheet is available but the master exists, use Treblo sentence timings where available; manually time only missing vocal boundaries that matter to the edit.

Import Treblo sentence timings through the local artist-site pipeline

Use the shared local pipeline in https://github.com/Maxwell-Pierce/artist-site-core. The core’s scripts/refresh_tracks.py decodes Treblo’s Next.js Flight payload and preserves each song’s alignedLyrics rows. Artist sites use that core as a pinned submodule; for a new production, reuse the existing checkout or clone the core into the project’s 07_Production folder and record its commit SHA. Do not mistake the core repo for the artist’s site or alter the artist site’s catalog just to time a song.

  1. Open the exact Treblo song URL through the available signed-in/local site session or local pipeline. Confirm song ID, title, version, artist attribution and duration against the supplied master. Record the URL, master filename, measured runtime and pipeline revision. An original and a cover/remix can have different lyrics, arrangement and timing; never transfer one version’s map to another.

  2. Prefer the artist site’s existing refreshed catalog when it already contains the exact song ID. The core’s scripts/refresh_tracks.py imports public playlists configured in the artist site’s catalog-source.json; its output preserves alignedLyrics in tracks.json. If the song is not in that catalog, use the same flight_models() / objects() parser from scripts/refresh_tracks.py on the exact Treblo song page payload and select the song object by its exact ID. Do not force a playlist edit or use the visible lyric panel as a substitute for timed source data.

  3. Copy each alignedLyrics item’s original text, start and end in elapsed seconds, in source order, preserving repeated lines as separate rows. Split section labels such as [Verse 1] into the section field for editing, but also retain the unchanged source string in source_text. Use timing_provenance=TREBLO_ALIGNED_LYRICS; use TREBLO_SENTENCE_TIMING_EXPORT for a timing export and MANUAL_AUDIO_CHECK only for a boundary manually timed against the local master. Do not round source decimals or fill gaps by inference.

  4. Save the source rows to 01_Audio_Lyrics/Treblo_Sentence_Timings_Source.csv with columns section,text,start_seconds,end_seconds,timing_provenance,source_text. Preserve the original Treblo response/export separately when practical. If the payload provides lyric text but no usable alignedLyrics or timing export, record the gap and manually time only the sentence boundaries needed for planned cues against the exact local master.

  5. Normalize using the reusable local helper at the edit’s actual integer frame rate. Until delivery settings are locked, 24 fps may be used as a clearly marked working assumption and regenerated later:

    python tools/normalize_treblo_sentence_timings.py --input Your_Project_Name_Production/01_Audio_Lyrics/Treblo_Sentence_Timings_Source.csv --output Your_Project_Name_Production/01_Audio_Lyrics/Treblo_Sentence_Timings.csv --song-url https://treblo.com/song/SONG_ID --fps 24

  6. Review source order, empty/overlapping intervals, unusually long line durations and the first, middle and final vocal anchors against the local master. Preserve questionable provider values in the source file; never silently repair it. If a material mismatch remains or the artist requests a rhythm-based timing pass, create a separate versioned Manual_Timing_Overrides_*.csv and applied revision. Record each original and proposed boundary, cue ID, basis (listening, artist timing, or BPM/phrase-length estimate), beat allocation, confidence and limitation. Label tempo-only estimates MANUAL_BPM_ESTIMATE_PROVISIONAL; reserve MANUAL_AUDIO_CHECK for boundaries actually checked by listening to the local master. Update the active cue map, storyboard and diagnostic overlay from the revision while keeping the Treblo source and prior edit maps intact. Keep elapsed source seconds authoritative for the source; label the selected active revision clearly.

  7. Transfer sentence IDs and intervals into the cue/tension map as vocal anchors. Decide each visual cut separately, then review the cut at normal speed with the final master. A sentence boundary is a planning anchor, not automatically a cut point.

The core also contains scripts/build_lyrics.py, scripts/align_words.py, scripts/enhance_lyrics.py and scripts/estimate_lyrics.py. These perform incremental CPU analysis, word alignment, Whisper/Demucs recognition and estimated word interpolation. They are not required to extract usable Treblo sentence intervals. Do not run the heavy recognition/estimation path when the matching Treblo alignedLyrics are present; never label estimated/interpolated words as Treblo timing. Use that path only to resolve a specific material gap, keep its output and provenance separate, and check disk space and local tool availability first.

Use the local master for final synchronization and beat/onset evidence for instrumental events. ASR is only a fallback for wording or missing/mismatched vocal passages; keep any recognition result separate and provisional until checked against the audio.

  1. Listen through for overall structure and feeling before assigning shots. Mark intro, verses, pre-choruses, choruses, instrumental changes, bridge and outro as present in this recording. Use the imported Treblo sentence intervals for vocal boundaries; do not run a second vocal-timing pass when those intervals match the master. Spot-check the version and decisive sync points only. Inspect pickups, held syllables, breaths, rests, offbeat accents and delivery changes when one affects a planned visual cue.
  2. Make a cue sheet with one row per meaningful phrase or instrumental event. Include cue ID, time in/out, lyric fragment or event, intended meaning, measured energy/acoustic event, interpreted tension, audible anchor, what the viewer should realize, provisional visual mode (story, performance/dance, or intercut), and eventual shot IDs. Give repeated lines separate cue IDs. A shot may span several lines and a single line may motivate several cuts. Choose the visual mode from the combined lyric, performance and tension reading; do not equate loudness with emotional intensity or automatically reserve dance for the loudest section.
  3. Decide what lands on each important cue. Choose the cut, the actor's decision, the moment the object is revealed, or the instant the camera comes to rest. For example, start a reach on the pickup and let the fingers release on the stressed word. Give the reaction the held vowel that follows.
  4. Mark intentional anticipation and delay. If the vocal names a loss, seeing the empty chair a beat before the word can build dread. Seeing it just after the word can feel like recognition. Choose one and record the reason.
  5. Check the entire timeline at the chosen frame rate. Use inclusive in / exclusive out intervals. Cover the beginning, silences, instrumental passages, tail and final frame. Count a dissolve's overlapping frames once in the edit. List deliberate black or held frames explicitly. If the mix changes later, update the cue sheet and affected shots before rendering.

Create the timecoded tension map before designing stills

Use the final stereo WAV or MP3 and matching lyric sheet. If aligned vocal or drum stems are supplied, use them to pinpoint masked syllables or hits, then confirm their positions against the master. Use the production timeline's frame rate and start time. Store elapsed audio seconds alongside editor timecode (HH:MM:SS:FF, with the appropriate drop-frame convention when applicable). Note any leading silence or timeline offset so the same cue lands on the same frame in the editor.

This is a required planning pass, not a post-stills analysis. Complete the provisional cue/tension map after verifying the song version and its usable lyric anchors, and before writing the treatment, choosing which visual mode carries each passage, or designing stills. The map should give the artist/director a first read on where the song holds back, gathers pressure, breaks open, and releases. Use that reading to propose story actions and allocate narrative coverage, performance/dance, or intercutting. For example, a restrained verse may stay in the story world, while a pre-chorus can begin cutting to small fragments of dance as anticipation rises; the chorus may release into full dance. These are creative options, not rules tied to measured loudness. Keep the curve provisional until the treatment and timed board can be heard against the master.

After the still-only animatic exists, review the planned curve against the actual image order and timing. A synchronized energy/tension diagnostic at that stage is a validation and revision tool: it can expose a mismatch or motivate a revised map, but it does not replace the pre-still analysis that informed the first story and image decisions. Preserve the prior map revision and record why any interpretation or cue timing changed.

Optional transcription and tool access

Speech recognition (for example, Whisper or another locally available transcriber) is an exception path, not a standard step. Use it only when Treblo timings are unavailable, materially incomplete, mismatched to the final master, or when lyric wording must be resolved. If Treblo sentence coverage is usable, skip transcription, ASR model setup, gap retries and ASR coverage-map generation. The supplied lyric sheet remains the text authority; the master recording remains the final sync authority. If a fallback is needed, keep its results separate and provisional until checked against the recording.

When the exception path is triggered, use transcription only if available through the current approved tools and environment. Check disk space before installing or caching anything. Do not change system permissions or request elevated access for optional analysis. If transcription is unavailable, restricted, too costly or unreliable on singing, listen to only the specific missing or disputed vocal interval in the final master and label the result MANUAL_AUDIO_CHECK. A tempo-only estimate is a separate fallback when the artist explicitly requests it; do not present it as audio-verified. Record the trigger, method, limitations and unresolved intervals in Production_Status.md. When Treblo coverage is usable and matches the master, record that ASR and manual vocal timing were skipped as unnecessary; no ASR artifacts are required.

Only when ASR is needed, save its raw segment timestamps and a separate audit such as ASR_Lyric_Anchor_Audit.csv in 01_Audio_Lyrics/. Keep recognized text beside the closest supplied-lyric passage, with an explicit match/review label and any remaining gaps. Do not replace lyric-sheet wording with recognition output or transfer uncertain segment edges directly into Tension_Map.csv. Keep already usable Treblo sentence intervals as the lyric anchors and process only the unresolved gaps.

Beat/onset detection for rhythmic cut timing

Run one beat/onset analysis pass for every production to map rhythmic and instrumental landmarks, even when Treblo sentence timing is complete. Decode a temporary copy of the master with FFmpeg and use an available local beat tracker or signal-analysis tool to estimate onset times and a provisional pulse grid. Prefer an already available local tool; do not install or cache another tracker when the first pass is clear enough for planning. If the first map is ambiguous or a rhythm-dependent cut needs stronger evidence, run an independent tracker such as aubio and compare candidate pulse grids. Do not change system Python or bypass environment controls. Save the method, source/version, timebase, confidence/limitations and provisional beat timestamps in Beat_Detection_Report.md and Beat_Grid_Provisional.csv; create Cut_Beat_Alignment_Provisional.csv only when competing pulse candidates need comparison.

Beat tracking supplies rhythmic landmarks, not lyric recognition, verified meter or automatically correct downbeats. Use Treblo sentence intervals for vocal phrase boundaries when they match the master; keep any necessary ASR or manual corrections separate and provenance-labeled. For a requested or clearly flagged BPM-based estimate, allocate whole or half beats to phrase-level cues, anchor to a known cue, and preserve expressive offbeat entrances where evidence supports them. Do not infer that the beat grid confirms sung attacks. Retiming one provider span can move all later cues, so recalculate downstream timings against phrase structure and the fixed song endpoint, then render a diagnostic overlay that distinguishes the estimate from provider timing. Mark it provisional until listened through against the local master. Use direct listening to decide which source cue governs each cut. Retain intentional anticipation, syncopation, held vowels and delayed reactions where they tell the story. Do not snap every cut to the nearest pulse. If an independent tracker is needed, compare candidates across sections because tempo changes, half/double-time readings, dense subdivisions and quiet passages can fool a global detector. Mark the beat map provisional until checked against the master.

Do not conflate the four audio layers: Treblo supplies sentence text and sentence intervals; beat/onset analysis suggests pulse and transient times; loudness/spectral features describe measured acoustic change; the tension map records the director's emotional interpretation. ASR is a fallback for wording or missing vocal passages. Each layer answers a different question. A quiet passage may carry high tension, and a detected beat may not be the right dramatic cut.

Fallback only: resolve a specific vocal gap when Treblo timing is unusable. Keep any first raw transcript. Run another model or targeted chunk only if the remaining gap affects a planned cue and the expected benefit justifies the work. Preserve each fallback's timestamps and settings separately. If a decoder rejects the audio, convert a temporary copy with FFmpeg; do not reinstall packages or change system permissions just to pursue an optional pass. Compare only the affected interval with supplied lyrics, flag uncertainty, and leave unresolved timing provisional. Do not run whole-track retries or gap audits when Treblo sentence coverage is usable.

Automated analysis can also summarize coarse energy, loudness, tempo, onset density, and likely arrangement changes. These measurements do not determine emotional tension: a quiet pause may raise anticipation while measured energy falls. Treat tension as the director's interpretation of the sound and lyric, keep it separate from measured energy, and verify every meaningful transition by listening to the master.

Session record example — Carry The Fire

For the supplied Carry The Fire master, FFprobe confirmed a 359.424-second, stereo, 48 kHz MP3. Cached faster-whisper-medium returned 44 segments after FFmpeg decoded mono 16 kHz PCM; cached faster-whisper-large-v2 returned 31 full-track segments with word timestamps. A prompted medium-model gap pass remained unreliable, so an additional large-v2 retry used independent chunks from 193.5–359.424 seconds with no lyric prompt and previous-text conditioning disabled. This unprompted pass independently corroborated a repeated pre-chorus-like vocal at about 217.6–243.2 seconds and bridge-like vocals around 292.5–305.0 seconds. Both large-v2 passes found no reliable words from about 243.4–292.9 seconds; after 305 seconds they returned only sparse musical symbols and one uncertain isolated word. ASR absence does not prove the track is instrumental. The artist directed that the recording is authoritative for timing and dramatic events, while the lyric sheet is story guidance and may vary. The broad event evidence and confidence limits are recorded in Audio_Event_Alignment_Provisional.csv; raw retry timestamps are in whisper_large_v2_gap_unprompted.json. Word timings remain navigation only, not forced alignment; manually check decisive cue boundaries before edit lock. The prior equal-allocation shot schedule is not an edit plan. Separately, two 180-row, 2-second summaries were produced: audio_energy_2s.csv records RMS loudness; Audio_Feature_Map.csv adds low-, mid- and high-band energy, spectral centroid, spectral flatness, and positive spectral flux. These are coarse acoustic measurements, not beat detection or emotional tension. The 1–5 tension curve is director-led and remains provisional until the musical sections and important transitions are checked against the master.

Carry The Fire beat pass (2026-11-20): the initial local FFmpeg + NumPy spectral-flux pass produced provisional periodicity candidates at 75.91, 103.57 and 161.39 BPM (the latter may be a subdivision); no tempo, pulse, meter or downbeat has been verified by listening. aubio 0.4.9 was installed in the current Windows user's Python 3.14 environment and run on a temporary 44.1 kHz mono PCM decode. It emitted 901 beats, reported 161.131 BPM at track end with 0.1516 confidence, and had a 157.61 BPM median inter-beat estimate. This supports auditioning the fast-pulse neighborhood but does not validate or replace the NumPy grid; retain both candidates for listening and do not automatically retime cuts. Full method, beat timestamps and script are in Beat_Detection_Report.md, Beat_Grid_Aubio_Provisional.csv and 05_Records/analyze_audio_beats_aubio.py.

Carry The Fire lyric-coverage visual (2026-11-20): ASR_Lyric_Coverage_Overview.png visualizes the full 359-second track, all 44 segments in the saved Whisper large-v2 lyric audit, candidate lyric comparisons and no-segment intervals. Thirty-eight segments are close-match candidates that still need listening; six are partial/misheard candidates. The companion ASR_Uncovered_Intervals_Provisional.csv records the gaps. Gray means no ASR segment was returned; it does not mean no vocal exists. The full wording, supplied-lyric comparisons and statuses remain in ASR_Lyric_Anchor_Audit.csv.

  1. Map the musical structure. Run one beat/onset pass and mark section boundaries, prominent beats and accents, relative loudness, arrangement builds, instrument entrances/dropouts, pauses and held notes. Listen around landmarks that will affect the edit. Use Treblo sentence intervals for vocal phrase anchors; do not align the lyrics again or time individual words/syllables unless Treblo data is missing or a specific cue cannot otherwise be resolved. Mark only unresolved fallback times provisional.
  2. Interpret the tension by phrase. Describe whether anticipation builds, holds, peaks or releases, and why. Consider vocal delivery, unresolved harmony, repetition, a delayed downbeat, lyrical meaning or an exposed silence. On the overview, draw musical energy and interpreted tension as separate tracks. A drum dropout may lower energy while anticipation rises. If using a 1–5 tension scale, anchor it to moments in this song and label it as the director's reading. Flag uncertain lyric alignments, ambiguous events and readings the artist may want to change.
  3. Give each important cue a visual consequence. Name what should land, down to the actor's gesture, camera stop, focus shift, reveal, hold or cut. Record its target timeline timecode and any intentional lead or lag in frames. More than one cue can belong to the same shot. Identify the pose and framing the opening still must provide, the state needed at the cut, and how much screen time the action needs. In the motion prompt, translate the target to seconds from the source clip's start, including its head handle. Check the actual rendered action against the music in the edit.

Save the audio-map deliverables in 01_Audio_Lyrics/. Write the existing cue sheet as Tension_Map.csv with one row per meaningful lyric phrase or instrumental event. Use a shared cue ID for related markers or shot references. Export Tension_Overview.png as a full-song timeline with section labels, energy changes, the interpreted tension curve and the IDs of the decisive moments. Keep precise timings in the CSV so the editor can recreate the markers.

If a fallback ASR pass is used across a substantial portion of the song and its coverage gaps affect planning, create ASR_Lyric_Coverage_Overview.png and ASR_Uncovered_Intervals_Provisional.csv. Do not generate a full coverage report for a small, resolved gap or when Treblo timings are usable. Any report must label missing model output as “no ASR segment returned,” not as proof of silence or an instrumental passage.

Illustrative cue at 24 fps, timeline starting at zero. In a phrase spanning 00:01:11:00–00:01:14:00, the last drum hit lands at 00:01:12:12 (72.500 s). The drums drop away and the next word enters at 00:01:13:06 (73.250 s). Mark the percussion dropout as a fall in energy and the exposed pause before the admission as rising tension. Start the shot with the character walking and enough lead room for a tracking move. Brake the camera on the last hit, hold the face through the 18-frame gap, then cut to the listener on the word. The two audible anchors each have a visual target with zero-frame offset. If the director wants the listener to anticipate the admission, move the cut two frames earlier and record that choice.

Before generating the first sequence's stills, scrub its decisive cue markers against the master and check that the planned opening poses leave room for the marked actions. If the recording changes, version the map and recheck the affected cues before animating those shots. With lyrics alone, label any preliminary ideas untimed and wait for the recording to produce the measured map.

Give the whole song a visual rhythm

Lay the song sections side by side. Under each, write the audience's new understanding, the performer's energy, the distance between viewer and subject, and the moment the section hands us into the next one. Read that row across the whole song. Decide where to withhold an image, where to let a phrase play in a single take, and where the visual peak belongs. A loud final chorus can carry a quiet close-up if the face delivers the payoff that the first chorus promised.

Use lyrics as subtext as well as subject matter. If the singer insists “I’m fine,” a person setting two places for dinner, then quietly returning one plate to the shelf, can carry the line. Test an image by asking what a viewer learns during it. If the answer repeats what the previous shot already taught, combine them or give the new shot a consequence. Let a recurring image gain meaning through its changed context.
