Prepare the hardest cut

Construct matching keyframes and write the motion prompt from the opening image.

Build a still and motion prompt in the lab

Check the hardest sequence before scaling up

Choose two or three consecutive shots the edit already needs. Include a difficult change such as wide to close, a reverse angle, two people exchanging an object, or a face emerging from shadow. Build their opening stills first. Ask whether the viewer can recognize the same people and place without the song or filenames, and whether the camera can make the planned move through that space. Resolve discrepancies in the master or the affected still before animating. Use these shots as the first motion pilot in section 8, selecting from contrasting song passages where possible.

Create one separate, full-resolution opening still per shot in the delivery aspect ratio. Give high-dependency shots a target end still as well. Add a midpoint reference for a demanding long take if the tool supports it. Where a precise cut depends on the end of a preceding render, treat the planned still as provisional and anchor the receiving shot to the accepted outgoing frame or its observable state. Return to master character references regularly to prevent accumulated drift.

Cut between cameras on the same action

Compare the performers' silhouettes across the whole phrase before locking the stills. Mark their level, supporting foot or hand, torso direction, arm shape and distance from one another. Give the next shot a distinct phase of the same movement, such as palm support, rise, turn, landing and recovery. Keep a repeated shape only when it is an intentional refrain. Check the actual edited entrances as well as the proposed stills, because the video model can return to a familiar pose.

Identify the movement that needs to finish in one shot. At 71 BPM an eight-beat phrase lasts about 6.76 seconds. Allow time for its approach and recovery when those are part of the action. Watch the take to choose the exit on the landing, weight transfer or release. Borrow time from a nearby insert to hold a strong performance. Lock each performer's wardrobe across locations, including sleeve length, trouser shape, footwear and reflections. Compare the opening, middle and exit of each generated take to the full-body reference.

Before designing adjacent stills, draw the line between the performers and choose a camera side. Compare the outgoing edit frame with the proposed incoming still side by side. Record camera position, shot size, direction of travel, supporting foot, hand contact and the phase of the gesture. A second camera should tell us something new. A side profile can reveal the turn of a shoulder, an overhead can show two paths separating, and a foot detail can carry the landing into a wide. As a starting point move the lens about 30 degrees or more around the subject, or change shot size decisively. Preserve the screen direction and eyeline across the cut. A closer crop from the same viewpoint can still reveal a jump if the action resets.

Cut on the moving part of the gesture. For example, leave the wide as her heel lifts and enter the side view as the toes push away. Keep the same foot and direction. If the generated hands or poses cannot match, bridge on a motivated moving detail, a passing shoulder or another dancer’s reaction, then rejoin the action. Reserve location dissolves for story transitions and use moving picture on both sides.

For continuous action, design the opening still halfway through a weight transfer, with the next movement physically available. In the prompt describe what the supporting foot, hips or hands are doing at time zero, what changes over the next few frames and what motion carries through the exit. Request enough extra source duration to discard the model’s startup and still fill the timeline slot plus transition handles. In the edit, watch the last half-second of the outgoing take and the first half-second of the incoming take as a loop at normal speed, then step through the cut. Choose the first usable frame pair showing the performer actually advancing. Camera drift, moving light and hair alone can conceal a stationary body. Record the chosen source in/out frames. When the take lacks handles, borrow adjacent coverage or shoot a pickup. Preserve intentional dramatic pauses where the cue calls for one.

Compose for the move. For an action that begins on screen, put the subject just before the decisive action. For continuous action, start inside the gesture. Leave lead room in the direction of travel, show clear space for hands and props, and place foreground, subject, and background at plausible distances. For a reveal, stage what initially hides the information and where it will be discovered. For a reaction, keep the eyes and the stimulus's direction readable. For a long tracking shot, draw the travel route and ending camera position before generating the first image.

Build a timed storyboard or animatic using these stills over the actual song, including planned holds and temporary cuts. Watch once muted for story and geography, then with audio for lyric impact. Review the hardest location change, biggest camera move and most subtle performance at their real edit lengths. If the story fails in stills, repair the cue, coverage or handoff here. Save and deliver the approved board and animatic.

Still-only MP4 animatic (required before motion generation)

After all numbered stills have passed the project's technical checks and the still sequence is ready for review, render a separate still-only MP4 using the approved stills, master song and current shot manifest. This is a story-and-timing review export, not a motion-generated film. Do not begin broad paid Kling motion generation until the artist/team has watched this animatic with sound and the critical timing/story problems have been corrected or explicitly logged as open.

  1. Check that local ffmpeg and ffprobe are available and record their versions. If missing, use an approved local installation path; do not silently install packages or replace source media. Keep the original master audio untouched.
  2. Derive each still's exact in/out time or frame count from the current edit map. Use one project frame rate consistently (for example, 24 fps if selected at intake); preserve shot order and intentional holds. Use hard cuts unless a named transition is part of the edit map. Do not assume every still gets equal screen time.
  3. Build the clean full-song H.264 MP4 at 1920×1080 with AAC audio, using the approved still files and original master audio. Start picture and audio at time zero; include the complete audio through its last decay. Record any frame-rate rounding or codec padding in the report rather than trimming the song to force a misleading exact duration.
  4. Render a second review MP4 from the clean animatic. Burn in a restrained, readable safe-area overlay for each shot with its shot_id, timeline in/out timecodes, sequence name and concise visual cue from the current manifest. Use the time range for that exact shot, change the overlay on the exact edit boundary, and keep it clear of faces and other critical story information. Keep the overlay review file separate from the clean animatic; never edit source stills to add labels.
  5. Render a separate diagnostic review MP4 from the clean animatic with a time-synchronized overlay on a transparent canvas. Show the active Treblo sentence anchor (or NO TREBLO SENTENCE INTERVAL — does not establish silence), a full-song progress trace with measured acoustic energy, and the editorial tension curve. Label measured energy and interpreted tension separately. When ASR fallback is used, distinguish matched, uncertain and uncovered lyric intervals; never imply that an unreturned ASR segment proves silence. Use compact translucent safe-area panels that keep the underlying still visible. Change active lyric and shot data on exact frame boundaries. Keep this diagnostic file separate from both the clean animatic and the shot-label review copy; do not edit source stills to add overlays.
  6. Save Stills_Animatic_Edit_Map.csv with shot ID, still path, audio time in/out, frame in/out, duration, cue IDs, transition/hold notes and timing status. Mark unverified lyric boundaries provisional.
  7. Render every still with an explicit project frame rate and exact frame count. Do not rely on the concat demuxer's default still-image frame rate or duration interpretation. For a 24 fps project, assign each still its exact integer video_frame_count from the edit map, set every image input to 24 fps, trim it to that frame count, reset timestamps, then concatenate in shot-ID/edit order. The sum of per-shot frame counts must equal the planned total before encoding. Keep hard-cut boundaries frame-exact; do not use frame duplication to conceal a mismatch. Record the exact render command/filter graph or use a reproducible script.
  8. Run decoded picture identity QC for every shot, especially rapid runs. After encoding, extract a frame from inside each shot (for example, its midpoint) and compare the decoded image region against that shot's referenced source still. Produce a CSV with shot ID, expected still path, sampled frame number/time, comparison result and measured difference. Investigate any unexpected duplicate/repeated frame, wrong still, stale image, black frame or identity mismatch before review. Inspect adjacent fast-cut shots side by side; changing overlay text is not proof that the underlying picture changed. Save a labeled contact sheet/proof for any corrected sequence.
  9. Validate all MP4s with ffprobe: dimensions, frame rate, video frame count, stream start times, audio sample rate/channels, per-stream durations and overall duration. Decode each complete file to a null output and inspect opening, middle, late and final shots, including whether labels and lyric/tension indicators change on the correct frame and remain readable without obscuring the image. Play all versions with the master song; automated metadata checks do not replace listening.
  10. Confirm each picture endpoint lands within one project frame of the intended audio endpoint, or record why not. Keep clean, shot-label and diagnostic-overlay animatics as separate versioned files; never overwrite source stills or audio. Log render settings, tool versions, output paths, duration and validation result in the production status. If any render or identity check fails, mark that export superseded and do not present it as the current review file.

Current project example (not universal settings): the Carry The Fire still animatic uses 73 approved 1920×1080 stills at 24 fps. Its clean MP4 is 06_Edit/Carry_The_Fire_Stills_Animatic_1920x1080.mp4; its corrected labeled review copy is 06_Edit/Carry_The_Fire_Stills_Animatic_1920x1080_Beat_Guess_v03_Review_Overlay.mp4. The older v02 review copy is superseded: its overlay advanced through shots 040–042 while repeating shot 040's picture. The corrected v03 render uses explicit per-shot frame trims and passed decoded midpoint identity checks for all 73 stills (see Shot_Frame_Identity_QC_Beat_Guess_v03.csv). The overlay displays shot ID, in/out timecodes, sequence and concise cue, and is burned only into the review copy. The corresponding timing record for this provisional version is 06_Edit/Stills_Animatic_Edit_Map_Beat_Guess_v03.csv; the original map remains preserved. Both videos are 8,626 frames (359.4167 s); the AAC stream is 359.400 s and the MP3 master is 359.424 s. This is within one 24 fps frame, but internal shot boundaries remain provisional wherever the audio analysis did not establish them. Incident lesson: a prior labeled review export advanced labels across shots 040–042 while repeating shot 040's underlying picture. A successful decode, correct total duration, and changing overlay did not catch this. Treat that render as superseded; require explicit per-still frame counts and decoded source-to-output identity QC for every shot on all future still animatics.

Automate local repeatable work. Use local scripts for map-to-frame conversion, source-preserving clip trims, music-track assembly, metadata/decode checks, task-result parsing, download manifests, and contact sheets. Keep model calls in the currently authorized service/CLI; local automation should not invent or replay remote jobs. Parse both direct and polled Kling result shapes, preserve task IDs and original work indexes, omit expiring signed media URLs from logs/CSV, and never treat an ambiguous response as permission to resubmit. Use temporary files plus atomic rename for downloads, verify each file with ffprobe, and keep raw generation sources separate from trimmed edit derivatives. Project example: Carry_The_Fire_Production/06_Edit/render_kling_pilot_v04.py, Carry_The_Fire_Production/06_Edit/render_full_kling_cut_v01.py, Carry_The_Fire_Production/06_Edit/merge_kling_prompt_packs.py, Carry_The_Fire_Production/06_Edit/submit_kling_motion_batch.py, and Carry_The_Fire_Production/05_Records/refresh_kling_motion_batch.py.

Discover the current account's available tools, models, reference roles, length increments, ratio, output resolution, sound controls, download options and costs. If the available mode accepts only a first frame, establish the exact cast, outfit, set and blocking in the approved opening still. If separate subject, scene or end-frame inputs are supported together, bind each file to its intended role and record the mapping. Use an end frame for a precise landing when it represents a physically reachable pose and camera position. Keep the actual settings, model version and reference versions with the prompt so a pickup can use the same setup.

KLING MOTION PROMPT • one shot
Open exactly on [first-frame file/role]. [Subject, starting stance and gaze].
On [visible trigger], [subject performs one specific action with direction,
effort, speed and prop contact]. [Partner or environment's physical response].
The camera starts [scale, height, position, perspective]. As [trigger], it
[single motivated path relative to subject, change of pace, focus target].
By [timing landmark], frame [precise end composition, pose, gaze, prop state].
Let [gesture or reaction] settle for the edit. Preserve [relevant identity,
wardrobe, geometry, screen direction, light]. [Specific constraint if needed.]

Convert musical triggers into observable cues and relative times when Kling receives only an image. State the song tempo and style (for example, “71 BPM, contemporary modern dance”), then translate them into visible action pacing: sustained phrases, approximate beat-count landmarks, controlled release and a settled endpoint. Tempo text is guidance, not beat synchronization. If the generation has no audio reference, do not claim that motion will land exactly on the song; align or slip the clip in the edit after listening. “At about 4 s she sees the train doors close and stops” is actionable. “Match the beat perfectly” requires a usable audio reference and still needs editorial verification. Give the rendered clip a concrete beginning, middle and end. Direct micro-performance through weight, eyes, breath, grip and hesitation. Use motion in clothing, reflections or dust only when it responds to the action. Clean the final prompt of storyboard shorthand, Markdown emphasis, alternate camera options, other shots' instructions and transitions that belong in the edit.

Before submitting, read the prompt while looking at the exact input frame. Point to the subject, the action's starting position, and the space the camera or performer will enter. Read it once at performance speed. A closed fist cannot release a letter already lying on a table. A tight profile cannot reveal a person behind the camera with a focus pull. Repair the still, camera path or coverage wherever the instructions conflict. Keep the prompt's priority clear enough that a successful take can be described in one observable sentence.
